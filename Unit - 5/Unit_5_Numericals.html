1Regression metrics

Four different summaries of the same set of residuals. They answer different questions, which is why all four exist.

All four at once
MAE = 1n ∑ |e|,  MSE = 1n ∑ e2,  RMSE = √MSE,  R2 = 1 − SSESST
where e = y − ŷ. RMSE is always at least MAE, and the gap tells you how much a few large errors dominate.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Tabulate y, ŷ, and the residual e = y − ŷ for every point.
  2. Add a column of |e| and a column of e2.
  3. MAE = (sum of |e|) / n.
  4. SSE = sum of e2; MSE = SSE / n; RMSE = √MSE.
  5. For R2: compute ȳ, then SST =  ∑ (y−ȳ)2.
  6. R2 = 1 − SSE/SST.
  7. Sanity checks: RMSE ≥ MAE always; R2 = 0 means you matched the mean; negative means you did worse.
Worked example — all four metrics on six predictions
Truth y = 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13. Predictions ŷ = 2.5, 5.5, 6, 10, 11.5, 12.
  1. Residuals e = y − ŷ+0.5, −0.5, +1.0, −1.0, −0.5, +1.0
  2. Absolute values and their sum0.5, 0.5, 1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 1.0 → sum = 4.5
  3. MAE4.56 = 0.7500
  4. Squares and their sum0.25, 0.25, 1.00, 1.00, 0.25, 1.00 → SSE = 3.75
  5. MSE and RMSEMSE = 3.756 = 0.6250;  RMSE = √0.6250 = 0.7906
  6. For R2, first the spread of y itselfȳ = 8 → SST = 25 + 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 + 25 = 70
  7. R21 − 3.7570 = 1 − 0.0536 = 0.9464
MAE 0.750, RMSE 0.791, R2 0.946. RMSE exceeds MAE because the two misses of 1.0 are squared before averaging. A large RMSE–MAE gap is a signal to look at the biggest residuals individually.
Practice
  1. Residuals are +2, −2, +2, −2. Compute MAE and RMSE. Why are they equal?
    MAE = 8/4 = 2.0. SSE = 16, MSE = 4, RMSE = 2.0. They are equal because every residual has the same magnitude — squaring cannot favour any one of them. This is the only case where RMSE = MAE.
  2. A model has SSE = 120 and SST = 100. What is R2, and what does it mean?
    R2 = 1 − 120/100 = −0.20. Negative means the model is worse than simply predicting the mean of y for every point. Something is badly wrong — a sign error, the wrong features, or evaluation on a different distribution.
  3. You are predicting house prices and one property is a ₹50 crore outlier. Should you report MAE or RMSE?
    Report both, and say why. RMSE will be dominated by that single property and will mostly measure how well you predicted it. MAE describes the typical listing far better. If the business cares about getting large valuations right, RMSE is the honest target; if it cares about the median listing, MAE is.

2Confusion matrix and classification metrics

A classifier is wrong in two different ways, and they rarely cost the same. The matrix separates them.

The four metrics
Accuracy = TP+TNall,  P = TPTP+FP,  R = TPTP+FN,  F1 = 2PRP+R
Precision has FP in its denominator, recall has FN. That single difference is the whole distinction between them.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Fix the threshold. Every number below depends on it.
  2. Flag every example scoring ≥ threshold.
  3. Among the flagged: those truly positive are TP, truly negative are FP.
  4. Among the not flagged: truly positive are FN, truly negative are TN.
  5. Check TP + FP + FN + TN equals the total.
  6. Substitute into each formula. Precision divides by what you flagged (TP+FP); recall divides by what was real (TP+FN).
  7. F1 last, from precision and recall.
Worked example — one threshold, four counts, four metrics
Twelve scored examples; six are genuinely positive. Scores with truth in brackets: 0.95(+) 0.90(+) 0.85(−) 0.80(+) 0.72(+) 0.65(−) 0.60(+) 0.55(−) 0.42(+) 0.35(−) 0.25(−) 0.10(−). Threshold = 0.60.
  1. Flag everything ≥ 0.60the top seven: 0.95, 0.90, 0.85, 0.80, 0.72, 0.65, 0.60
  2. Split the flagged by true labelpositive: 0.95, 0.90, 0.80, 0.72, 0.60 → TP = 5;  negative: 0.85, 0.65 → FP = 2
  3. Split the five not flaggedpositive but missed: 0.42 → FN = 1;  correctly ignored: 0.55, 0.35, 0.25, 0.10 → TN = 4
  4. Check5 + 2 + 1 + 4 = 12 ✓
  5. Accuracy5 + 412 = 912 = 0.750
  6. Precision and recallP = 55+2 = 0.714;  R = 55+1 = 0.833
  7. F12 · 0.714 × 0.8330.714 + 0.833 = 2 · 0.5951.547 = 0.769
Accuracy 0.750, precision 0.714, recall 0.833, F1 0.769. At the default threshold of 0.50 the same model scores 0.667 / 0.625 / 0.833 / 0.714 — worse on three of the four. The model never changed; only the cut did.
Practice
  1. A fraud detector sees 10 000 transactions, 100 of them fraudulent. It flags nothing at all. What is its accuracy, and its recall?
    TP = 0, FP = 0, FN = 100, TN = 9900. Accuracy = 9900/10000 = 0.99. Recall = 0/(0+100) = 0.00. A 99% accurate model that catches zero fraud — which is exactly why accuracy is the wrong metric here.
  2. TP = 40, FP = 10, FN = 20, TN = 30. Compute precision, recall and F1.
    P = 40/50 = 0.80; R = 40/60 = 0.667; F1 = 2(0.80)(0.667)/(0.80+0.667) = 1.0667/1.4667 = 0.727. Accuracy would be 70/100 = 0.70.
  3. Screening for a serious disease: which matters more, precision or recall, and what does that do to the threshold?
    Recall. A false negative means a sick patient is sent home untreated; a false positive means an unnecessary follow-up test. The first is far worse, so you lower the threshold to catch more positives, accepting more false alarms. Precision falls, recall rises — a deliberate, defensible trade.

3ROC and AUC

Sweep the threshold across its whole range and you get a curve that judges the model rather than any one cut.

The two axes
TPR = TPTP + FN,   FPR = FPFP + TN
Plot TPR (vertical) against FPR (horizontal) as the threshold falls from above every score to below every score.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Sort the examples by score, highest first.
  2. Start with the threshold above every score: TPR = 0, FPR = 0 — the origin.
  3. Lower the threshold past one example at a time.
  4. If that example is a true positive, the curve steps up by 1/P.
  5. If it is a negative, the curve steps right by 1/N.
  6. You finish at (1, 1).
  7. AUC = the area underneath, computed as a sum of rectangles and triangles — or equivalently, the probability a random positive outscores a random negative.
Worked example — tracing the curve and computing AUC
The same twelve examples: P = 6 positives, N = 6 negatives. Each upward step is 1/6 = 0.167; each rightward step is also 1/6.
  1. Threshold above everything(FPR, TPR) = (0, 0)
  2. Past 0.95 (+) and 0.90 (+)two positives → two steps up → (0, 0.333)
  3. Past 0.85 (−)a negative → step right → (0.167, 0.333)
  4. Past 0.80 (+) and 0.72 (+)two more up → (0.167, 0.667)
  5. Past 0.65 (−), 0.60 (+), 0.55 (−), 0.42 (+)right, up, right, up → (0.500, 1.000) — all positives now caught
  6. The remaining three negativesthree steps right along the top → (1, 1)
  7. Area, as columns of width 0.1670.167(0.333) + 0.167(0.667) + 0.167(0.833) + 0.167(1) + 0.167(1) + 0.167(1) = 0.806
AUC = 0.806. Cross-check by the ranking definition: of the 36 positive–negative pairs, the positive outscores the negative in 29 of them, and 29/36 = 0.806. Both routes agree, which is the standard way to verify an AUC by hand.
Practice
  1. What AUC does a model that guesses at random achieve, and why?
    0.5. Its ROC curve is the diagonal from (0,0) to (1,1), and the area under that line is half the unit square. Equivalently, a random positive outscores a random negative exactly half the time.
  2. Model A has AUC 0.92; Model B has AUC 0.78. Is A always the better choice?
    Not necessarily. AUC averages performance over all thresholds, including ones you would never use. If you must operate at a very low false-positive rate, B may beat A in that narrow region while losing overall. Always check performance at your actual operating point.
  3. A model achieves AUC = 1.0 on your test set. What should you suspect?
    Leakage. Perfect separation on real data is extremely rare. The usual causes are a feature that encodes the target (an ID assigned after the outcome, a post-event timestamp), or test rows that also appear in training. Check the feature list and the split before celebrating.

4Cross validation and hyperparameter search

One split gives one number that depends on luck. Cross validation removes the luck and tells you how much to trust the result.

Mean and spread
CV = 1kk∑i=1 si,   sd = √1k ∑ (si−̄s)2
Report both. The mean is your estimate; the spread tells you whether a rival model's score is a real difference or noise.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Shuffle, then cut the data into k equal folds (k = 5 or 10).
  2. For each fold: train on the other k − 1, test on this one, record the score.
  3. Every row is tested on exactly once and trained on k − 1 times.
  4. Mean of the k scores = the reported performance.
  5. Standard deviation = how much it wobbles.
  6. For classification use stratified folds so each keeps the class balance.
  7. Fit scalers and feature selection inside each fold, never before splitting.
  8. Grid search cost = (number of combinations) × k fits.
Worked example — 5-fold CV, then the cost of a grid search
A model is cross-validated over five folds of 4 rows each.
  1. The five fold scores0.82, 0.88, 0.79, 0.91, 0.85
  2. Mean0.82+0.88+0.79+0.91+0.855 = 4.255 = 0.8500
  3. Deviations from the mean−0.03, +0.03, −0.06, +0.06, 0.00
  4. Standard deviation√0.0009+0.0009+0.0036+0.0036+05 = √0.00180 = 0.0424
  5. What that meansa single split could have returned 0.79 or 0.91 purely by luck of the draw
  6. Now cost a grid search: 4 × 5 × 3 hyperparameter values60 combinations × 5 folds = 300 model fits
  7. At 40 seconds per fit300 × 40 s = 12 000 s = 3 h 20 min
0.850 ± 0.042. A rival model scoring 0.87 on one split is inside the noise of this measurement — you have not shown it is better. Reporting the spread is what stops you chasing differences that are not there.
Practice
  1. Fold scores are 0.70, 0.90, 0.71, 0.89, 0.70. Mean and sd? What does the spread suggest?
    Mean = 3.90/5 = 0.780. Deviations −0.08, +0.12, −0.07, +0.11, −0.08 → sd = √0.00946 ≈ 0.097. That is a large spread — performance depends heavily on which rows are held out, suggesting too little data or a very unstable model.
  2. You standardise the whole dataset, then run 5-fold CV. What is wrong?
    Data leakage. The mean and standard deviation used for scaling were computed using the test folds, so information from the held-out data reached the training step. The reported score will be optimistic, and nothing downstream will reveal it. Fit the scaler inside each fold.
  3. Grid search over 3 hyperparameters with 5, 4 and 3 values, under 10-fold CV. How many model fits?
    5 × 4 × 3 = 60 combinations, × 10 folds = 600 fits. Adding a fourth hyperparameter with 4 values would make it 2 400 — which is why random search is preferred past two or three parameters.
