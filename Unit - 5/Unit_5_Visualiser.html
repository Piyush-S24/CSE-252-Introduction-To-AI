1Regression performance metrics

The model predicted ŷ, the truth was y. Every regression metric is a different way of summarising the gap.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

You cannot improve what you cannot measure. Once the model makes predictions you need one number that says how wrong it is — so you can compare two models, decide whether yesterday's change helped, and know when to stop. Without that number, “is it good?” has no answer and every decision becomes opinion.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Just look at the predictions” works for six rows and fails for six thousand. And eyeballing cannot rank two models that are both roughly right. “Just use one metric” is the other trap: MAE, RMSE and R² answer genuinely different questions, which is why all three exist rather than one.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You ship the wrong model. A change that looks better on three examples you happened to check can be worse overall, and you will not find out until it is in use. Worse, with no agreed metric two people on the same team can both be “right” about which model to deploy.

MAE
MAE = 1nn∑i=1 |yi − ŷi|
Mean absolute error. The average size of the miss, in the same units as y. Every error counts in proportion to how big it is.
MSE and RMSE
MSE = 1nn∑i=1 (yi − ŷi)2,    RMSE = √MSE
Mean squared error punishes a big miss far more than several small ones, because the error is squared. RMSE takes the square root to put the number back into the units of y so it can be compared with MAE.
R²
R2 = 1 − SSESST = 1 −  ∑  (yi −ŷi)2 ∑  (yi −ȳ)2
Coefficient of determination. It compares your model against the dumbest possible one — always predicting the mean. R2 = 1 is perfect, 0 means you did no better than the mean, and negative is possible and means you did worse.

Why RMSE is always at least MAE
Squaring magnifies the large errors before averaging, so the two can only be equal when every error has the same magnitude. The gap between them is therefore a signal in its own right: RMSE much larger than MAE means a few big misses are dominating, and those are usually worth looking at individually.
Worked example — computing all four metrics on six predictions
Truth y = 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 and predictions ŷ = 2.5, 5.5, 6, 10, 11.5, 12.
  1. Residuals e = y − ŷ+0.5, −0.5, +1.0, −1.0, −0.5, +1.0
  2. MAE — average absolute residual0.5 + 0.5 + 1.0 + 1.0 + 0.5 + 1.06 = 4.56 = 0.7500
  3. Square each residual and sum0.25 + 0.25 + 1.0 + 1.0 + 0.25 + 1.0 = SSE = 3.75
  4. MSE and RMSEMSE = 3.756 = 0.6250,  RMSE = √0.6250 = 0.7906
  5. For R2 we need the spread of y itself̄y = 8,  SST = 25 + 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 + 25 = 70
  6. R2 compares your error against predicting the mean1 − 3.7570 = 0.9464
MAE 0.750, RMSE 0.791, R2 0.946. RMSE exceeds MAE because the two misses of 1.0 are squared before averaging — that gap is a signal that a few large errors dominate, and it is worth looking at them individually.

2Confusion matrix and classification metrics

A classifier outputs a score. You choose a threshold. That one choice decides every number in the table below — so quoting a metric without its threshold says very little.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A classifier can be wrong in two different ways, and they almost never cost the same. Missing a disease is not the same as a false alarm; blocking a real customer is not the same as letting fraud through. The confusion matrix separates those two mistakes so you can see which one you are actually making.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Just use accuracy” is the single most common beginner mistake. If 1% of transactions are fraud, a model that says “never fraud” scores 99% accuracy and catches nothing at all. Accuracy hides this completely, because it counts the 99% it got right for free. Precision and recall cannot hide it, because they only look at the class you care about.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You celebrate a useless model. Accuracy 99% goes in the report, the system goes live, and it never flags a single fraudulent transaction — and because accuracy is high, nobody looks again for months.

Accuracy
Accuracy = TP + TNTP + TN + FP + FN
Fraction of all predictions that were right. Badly misleading when the classes are imbalanced.
F1 score
F1 = 2 ·P · RP + R
The harmonic mean of precision and recall — high only when both are high.
Precision
Precision = TPTP + FP
Of everything you flagged, how much was real? Low precision means false alarms.
Recall
Recall = TPTP + FN
Of everything that was real, how much did you catch? Low recall means misses.

Twelve scored examples — drag the threshold

Six are genuinely positive and six are not. Move the threshold and watch every number move with it.

threshold =0.50
Precision and recall pull against each other
Lower the threshold and you catch more positives (recall rises) but flag more negatives too (precision falls). There is no setting that maximises both, so the right threshold depends on which mistake costs more. Screening for a disease: a miss is far worse than a false alarm, so favour recall. Flagging an account as fraudulent and freezing it: a false alarm has a real cost, so favour precision.

ROC and AUC — judging the model, not the threshold

Sweep the threshold from high to low and plot true-positive rate against false-positive rate. The area under that curve is a single number that does not depend on any one threshold.

step 0 of 0
What AUC actually means
AUC is exactly the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect separation. It is useful precisely because it measures whether the model ranks well, separately from where you decide to cut.
Worked example — one threshold, four counts, four metrics
Twelve scored examples; six are genuinely positive. Scores (truth in brackets): 0.95(+) 0.90(+) 0.85(−) 0.80(+) 0.72(+) 0.65(−) 0.60(+) 0.55(−) 0.42(+) 0.35(−) 0.25(−) 0.10(−). Take threshold 0.60.
  1. Flag everything scoring ≥ 0.60that is the top seven: 0.95, 0.90, 0.85, 0.80, 0.72, 0.65, 0.60
  2. Split those seven by their true labelgenuinely positive: 0.95, 0.90, 0.80, 0.72, 0.60 → TP = 5; actually negative: 0.85, 0.65 → FP = 2
  3. Now the five not flaggedpositive but missed: 0.42 → FN = 1; correctly ignored: 0.55, 0.35, 0.25, 0.10 → TN = 4
  4. AccuracyTP + TN12 = 5 + 412 = 0.750
  5. Precision — of what I flagged, how much was real?55 + 2 = 0.714
  6. Recall — of what was real, how much did I catch?55 + 1 = 0.833
  7. F1 — the harmonic mean of the two2 · 0.714 × 0.8330.714 + 0.833 = 0.769
Move the threshold to the default 0.50 and the same model scores accuracy 0.667, precision 0.625, recall 0.833, F1 0.714 — worse on three of four. The model never changed; only where you cut did.

3Cross validation

One train/test split gives you one number, and that number depends on which rows happened to land in the test set. Cross validation removes that luck.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A single train/test split gives you one number, and that number depends on which rows happened to land in the test set. Cross validation runs the experiment several times with different rows held out and averages the results, so the score you report is about the model rather than about a lucky split.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Just make the test set bigger” sounds reasonable and is worse on both counts: every row you move into the test set is a row the model no longer learns from, and you still get only one estimate with no idea how much it would wobble. Cross validation uses every row for training some of the time and testing exactly once, and hands you a spread as well as a mean.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You report 0.91 when the honest answer was “somewhere between 0.79 and 0.91”. Then you pick between two models whose real difference is smaller than the noise in your own measurement — so you are choosing at random while believing you are choosing on evidence.

5-fold cross validation

Split the data into k equal folds. Train on k − 1 of them and test on the one left out. Do that k times, so every row is tested on exactly once, and average the scores.

step 0 of 0
What to report
CV score = 1kk∑i=1 scorei,    spread = std(score1, …, scorek)
Report both. The mean is your estimate of performance; the spread tells you how much to trust it. A mean of 0.85 with a spread of 0.02 is a different situation from a mean of 0.85 with a spread of 0.15.
VariantWhen to use it
k-foldthe default; k = 5 or 10
Stratified k-foldclassification — keeps each fold's class balance the same as the whole dataset. Use this by default for classification.
Leave-one-outvery small datasets; k = n, so it is accurate but costs n fits
Time series splitordered data — always train on the past and test on the future, never shuffle
The leak that invalidates everything
Fit your scaler, your feature selection and your imputation inside each fold, on the training part only. Scaling the whole dataset before splitting lets the test fold's statistics reach the training step, and the score you get back will be optimistic in a way nothing downstream will reveal.
Worked example — 5-fold cross validation, and why the spread matters
20 rows, shuffled, cut into 5 folds of 4. The model is trained five times, each time holding out a different fold.
  1. Fold 1 — train on rows 5–20, test on rows 1–4score 0.82
  2. Fold 2 — train on 1–4 and 9–20, test on 5–8score 0.88
  3. Folds 3, 4 and 5 the same wayscores 0.79, 0.91, 0.85
  4. Mean — this is the number to report0.82 + 0.88 + 0.79 + 0.91 + 0.855 = 4.255 = 0.8500
  5. Standard deviation — this is whydeviations −0.03, +0.03, −0.06, +0.06, 0.00 → √0.0018 × 55 = 0.0424
  6. Read what that tells youa single train/test split could have handed you 0.79 or 0.91 purely by luck of the draw
0.850 ± 0.042. If a rival model scores 0.87 on one split, that is inside the noise of this measurement — you have not shown it is better. Reporting the spread is what stops you chasing differences that are not there.

4Hyperparameter tuning

Parameters are learned from the data. Hyperparameters are the ones you choose before training starts — and they have to be chosen by search.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Hyperparameters are the settings you choose before training — how deep the tree, how many neighbours, how strong the regularisation. The library's defaults were chosen for some other dataset, not yours, and the right value genuinely cannot be derived — it has to be found by trying. A systematic search finds it and records what it tried.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Just try a few by hand” feels faster and loses twice: it is not reproducible, so nobody (including you, next month) can tell how the final value was chosen, and people stop as soon as a number looks acceptable rather than when it stops improving. “Just use the defaults” is a reasonable starting point and a poor finishing one — it usually leaves real accuracy unclaimed.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Two different failures. Skip tuning and you ship a model worse than the one you could have had for an hour of compute. Tune on the test set — the more dangerous mistake — and the test score stops being an estimate of unseen performance at all, because you have now fitted your choices to it.

Grid search

Try every combination on a grid you specify. Exhaustive and reproducible, but the cost multiplies with every hyperparameter you add — 4 values of 3 hyperparameters is already 64 fits, times k for cross validation.

Random search

Sample combinations at random from ranges you specify. For the same budget it usually finds a better setting, because real problems have a few hyperparameters that matter a lot and several that barely matter at all.

Why random search wins on the same budget

Both panels spend nine trials. The vertical axis is a hyperparameter that barely affects the score; the horizontal one matters a great deal. Grid search tests only three distinct values of the one that matters. Random search tests nine.

The split you need three of
Tune on a validation set, never the test set. The moment you choose a hyperparameter because it scored well on the test set, that score stops being an estimate of unseen performance. The clean arrangement is train / validation / test, or cross validation on the training portion with the test set locked away until the very end.
Worked example — counting the real cost of a grid search
Three hyperparameters to tune, with 4, 5 and 3 candidate values, evaluated under 5-fold cross validation. One model fit takes 40 seconds.
  1. Count the grid points4 × 5 × 3 = 60 combinations
  2. Each combination is scored by cross validation, not a single fit60 × 5 folds = 300 model fits
  3. Total time300 × 40 s = 12 000 s = 3 hours 20 minutes
  4. Now add one more hyperparameter with 4 values240 combinations × 5 = 1200 fits = 13 hours 20 minutes — four times the cost for one extra knob
  5. Random search with the same 300-fit budget60 random draws × 5 folds, but each draw samples all three hyperparameters at a fresh value
  6. Why that winsgrid search tests only 4 distinct values of the first hyperparameter (each repeated 15 times); random search tests up to 60
Same compute, 15× the coverage of whichever hyperparameter turns out to matter. This is why random search is the usual default once you have more than two parameters — and why the grid is reserved for a final narrow sweep.

5Introduction to Explainable AI

A model that is accurate but unexplainable is unusable in medicine, lending and recruitment — not because the accuracy is doubted, but because a decision that affects someone has to be defensible.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Accuracy tells you how often the model is right. It never tells you what the model is using. The moment a prediction affects a person — a loan, a diagnosis, a shortlist — somebody will ask why, and “the model said so” is not an answer you can give a customer, a regulator or a court.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“If the accuracy is high, who cares how it works?” fails for three practical reasons, none of them philosophical. You cannot debug a model whose reasoning you cannot see. You cannot detect proxy bias — a model using postcode as a stand-in for income looks fine on every aggregate metric. And in regulated domains you cannot deploy it, because explanation is a legal requirement rather than a preference.

What goes wrong if you skip it

The model learns the wrong thing and you never find out. The textbook case: a pneumonia classifier scored brilliantly because it had learned to recognise the portable X-ray machine used on the sickest patients. Accuracy said it worked. An explanation would have shown it was reading the scanner, not the lungs — and that it would fail completely the moment the hospital changed equipment.

Global explanation

How does the model behave in general? Feature importance, partial dependence. Answers "what drives this model's decisions?"

Local explanation

Why this prediction, for this person? LIME and SHAP. Answers "why was my loan refused?" — which is the question that actually gets asked, and the one regulation tends to require.

A local explanation, read as a balance

One applicant, one decision. Each bar is how much a feature pushed the model toward approving or refusing, starting from the average prediction.

Importance is not causation
A feature can be important to the model simply because it correlates with something that matters. Postcode may rank high not because geography causes default but because it stands in for income — which is exactly how a model becomes discriminatory while every individual step looks reasonable. An explanation tells you what the model used, never what is true.
Worked example — reading a local explanation for one refused application
The model's average prediction across all applicants is 0.50. For this applicant the explanation attributes the following contributions.
  1. Start from the base rate0.50 — what you would predict knowing nothing
  2. Add the factors pushing toward approvalcredit score 710: +0.21,  income ₹9.2 L: +0.14,  age 34: +0.02  →  total +0.37
  3. Add the factors pushing against3 existing loans: −0.18,  employment 14 months: −0.11,  requested ₹18 L: −0.09  →  total −0.38
  4. Sum to the final score0.50 + 0.37 − 0.38 = 0.49
  5. Compare against the approval line0.49 < 0.50 → refused, but only just
  6. Identify the single biggest factor againstthree existing loans, at −0.18
That last line is the whole point: “the main factor was your three existing loans” is a sentence you can give the applicant. “The model scored you 0.49” is not — and in regulated lending, is not legally sufficient either.
