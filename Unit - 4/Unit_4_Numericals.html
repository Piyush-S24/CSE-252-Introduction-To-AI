1Distance, and why scaling comes first

Every algorithm in this unit reduces to one question: how far apart are these two points?

Euclidean distance in 2-D
d(p, q) = √(p1−q1)2 + (p2−q2)2
Keep the squared value while ranking — the ordering is identical and you save the square roots.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Subtract coordinate by coordinate.
  2. Square each difference and add them up.
  3. Take the square root — or skip it if you only need to rank.
  4. Before any of this, check the features are on comparable scales. If not, standardise: z = (x − μ) / σ.
  5. Rule of thumb: if one feature's range is more than ~10× another's, scaling is not optional.
Worked example — what happens when you forget to scale
Two customers. A = (salary ₹30 000, experience 2 years), B = (₹32 000, 15 years), C = (₹80 000, 3 years). Which is nearer to A?
  1. Unscaled distance A to B√(30000−32000)2 + (2−15)2 = √4 000 000 + 169 ≈ 2000.04
  2. Unscaled distance A to C√(30000−80000)2 + (2−3)2 = √2 500 000 000 + 1 ≈ 50 000
  3. Unscaled verdictB is nearer — but the 13-year experience gap contributed only 169 out of 4 000 169, i.e. 0.004% of the distance
  4. Now standardise. Salary: μ = 47 333, σ ≈ 22 509A → −0.77, B → −0.68, C → +1.45
  5. Experience: μ = 6.67, σ ≈ 5.86A → −0.80, B → +1.42, C → −0.63
  6. Scaled distance A to B√(0.09)2 + (2.22)2 = 2.22
  7. Scaled distance A to C√(2.22)2 + (0.17)2 = 2.23
Unscaled, B looked 25× closer than C. Scaled, they are essentially equidistant — and for entirely different reasons. The unscaled answer was really just “whose salary is nearest”.
Practice
  1. Compute the distance between (1, 5) and (4, 1).
    √(1−4)² + (5−1)² = √9 + 16 = √25 = 5 — a 3-4-5 triangle.
  2. Which is closer to (0,0): P = (3, 4) or Q = (5, 1)?
    Squared distances: P → 9+16 = 25; Q → 25+1 = 26. P is closer. Note the square roots were never needed.
  3. A dataset has age (18–65) and income (₹2 L–₹50 L). Will k-Means clusters reflect age at all?
    No. Income differences are in lakhs while age differences are in tens, so the squared income term dominates every distance by a factor of around 108. The clusters will be income bands with age playing no part. Standardise both features first.

2k-Means

Assign every point to its nearest centre, move each centre to the mean of what it got, repeat until nothing moves.

The two steps
assign: x → argmink ‖x − μk‖,    update: μk = 1|Ck| ∑x ∈ Ck x
These two steps alternate. Each one can only lower the SSE, which is why the algorithm always terminates.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Place k initial centres (given, or chosen at random).
  2. Assign: for every point compute the distance to each centre and attach it to the nearest. Keep distances squared.
  3. Update: replace each centre by the coordinate-wise mean of its members.
  4. Compare the new centres with the old. If unchanged, stop.
  5. Otherwise repeat from the assign step.
  6. Report the final SSE = sum of squared distances from each point to its own centre.
  7. If a tie occurs, state your tie-break rule and apply it consistently.
Worked example — one full iteration, by hand
Seven points A(1,1) B(1.5,2) C(3,4) D(5,7) E(3.5,5) F(4.5,5) G(3.5,4.5), with k = 2 and centres started on A and D.
  1. Distances from C(3,4) to each centreto μ1(1,1): √4 + 9 = 3.606;  to μ2(5,7): √4 + 9 = 3.606
  2. C is exactly tiedstate the rule — ties go to the lower-numbered cluster → C joins cluster 1
  3. Assign the restcluster 1 = {A, B, C};  cluster 2 = {D, E, F, G}
  4. Update centre 1μ1 = (1 + 1.5 + 33, 1 + 2 + 43) = (1.833, 2.333)
  5. Update centre 2μ2 = (5 + 3.5 + 4.5 + 3.54, 7 + 5 + 5 + 4.54) = (4.125, 5.375)
  6. The centres moved, so iterate againon the next pass C switches to cluster 2, and the centres settle at (1.25, 1.5) and (3.9, 5.1)
  7. Final SSE8.525
Final clusters {A, B} and {C, D, E, F, G}, SSE = 8.525. Point C changed cluster between iterations — the first assignment is never the final one, which is why the loop exists.
Practice
  1. Points (2,2), (3,3), (8,8), (9,9) with centres at (2,2) and (9,9). Assign, then update.
    (2,2) and (3,3) are nearer (2,2); (8,8) and (9,9) are nearer (9,9). New centres: (2.5, 2.5) and (8.5, 8.5). A second pass changes nothing, so it has converged. SSE = 0.5+0.5+0.5+0.5 = 2.0.
  2. Two runs of k-Means on the same data give SSE 14.2 and SSE 11.8. Which do you keep, and why do they differ?
    Keep 11.8 — lower SSE is a better local optimum at this k. They differ because k-Means converges to a local minimum that depends on the random initialisation. This is exactly why multiple restarts (or k-means++) are standard practice.
  3. Why can you not choose k by picking whichever value minimises SSE?
    Because SSE always falls as k rises, reaching 0 when every point is its own cluster. Minimising it would always return k = n. You need the elbow, or silhouette, which balances tightness against separation.

3Hierarchical clustering

Start with every point alone, repeatedly merge the two closest clusters, and record the height at which each merge happened.

Linkage rules
single: d(A, B) = min d(a, b),   complete: d(A, B) = max d(a, b)
Average linkage uses the mean over all cross pairs; Ward uses the increase in SSE caused by merging. The linkage must be stated — different linkages give different trees.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Compute the full distance matrix between all n points.
  2. Find the smallest off-diagonal entry; merge those two clusters. Record the distance as the merge height.
  3. Recompute distances from the new cluster to every other cluster, using your linkage rule.
  4. Repeat until one cluster remains. There will be exactly n − 1 merges.
  5. To read off k clusters, cut the dendrogram horizontally so that it crosses k vertical lines.
  6. A cut through a tall gap is a more defensible clustering than one through a crowded region.
Worked example — single and complete linkage diverge
P(7, 3.5) Q(9.5, 5) R(5, 9.5) S(5, 1.5) T(2, 4.5). Key distances: d(P,S) = 2.828, d(P,Q) = 2.915, d(T,S) = 4.243, d(P,T) = 5.099, d(Q,S) = 5.701, d(Q,R) = 6.364, d(T,R) = 5.831, d(R,S) = 8.000.
  1. Step 1 — both linkages merge the globally closest pairmin over all pairs is d(P, S) = 2.828 → form (PS)
  2. Single linkage, step 2 — nearest member winsd(Q, (PS)) = min(2.915, 5.701) = 2.915, the smallest remaining → Q joins → (Q(PS))
  3. Complete linkage, step 2 — furthest member countsd(Q, (PS)) = max(2.915, 5.701) = 5.701, but d(T, (PS)) = max(5.099, 4.243) = 5.099 is smaller → T joins instead
  4. Single linkage, remaining mergesT joins at 4.243, then R at 5.831 — one point at a time, a chain
  5. Complete linkage, remaining mergesQ and R merge separately at 6.364, then the two groups join at 8.000
  6. Cut each tree for k = 2single → {P,S,Q,T} and {R};  complete → {T,P,S} and {Q,R}
Same points, two different answers. Single linkage chained; complete linkage produced two balanced groups. Quoting “the hierarchical clustering” without naming the linkage is meaningless.
Practice
  1. Four points with d(A,B)=2, d(A,C)=6, d(A,D)=9, d(B,C)=5, d(B,D)=8, d(C,D)=3. Perform single-linkage clustering.
    Smallest is d(A,B)=2 → merge (AB) at 2. Next smallest overall is d(C,D)=3 → merge (CD) at 3. Finally d((AB),(CD)) = min(6,9,5,8) = 5. Tree: ((AB) at 2, (CD) at 3, joined at 5).
  2. For the same data, what does complete linkage give for the final merge height?
    (AB) and (CD) still form at 2 and 3 (single points, so the rule does not bite yet). The final merge uses max(6, 9, 5, 8) = 9 instead of 5. Same tree shape, different heights — and with more points the shape would change too.
  3. Why does hierarchical clustering not scale to a million points?
    The distance matrix alone is n2 entries — for n = 106 that is 1012 numbers, several terabytes. Time is O(n2 log n) at best. k-Means is O(nkt) and scales; this does not.

4DBSCAN

A cluster is a dense region. No k to choose, any shape allowed, and points in no dense region are labelled noise.

The two definitions
Nε(p) = [q : d(p, q) ≤ ε],   p is core ⇔ |Nε(p)| ≥ MinPts
The neighbourhood includes the point itself. With MinPts = 3 a core point needs two other points within ε. This is the single most common slip.
The method — what to actually do
  1. For every point, list all points within ε — including itself.
  2. If |N| ≥ MinPts, mark it core.
  3. For each non-core point, check whether any core point lies within ε. If yes it is a border point; if no it is noise.
  4. Join core points that are within ε of each other into the same cluster.
  5. Attach each border point to the cluster of a core point that reaches it.
  6. A border point joins a cluster but cannot be used to extend it — expansion only ever happens from core points.
Worked example — classifying eight points
A(8, 5.5) B(1.5, 8) C(8.5, 5) D(8.5, 3.5) E(7.5, 6) F(1.5, 9) G(2, 8.5) H(3.5, 2), with ε = 1.5 and MinPts = 3.
  1. Neighbourhood of Ad(A,C) = √0.25 + 0.25 = 0.707 ≤ 1.5 ✓, d(A,E) = 0.707 ✓, plus A itself → N(A) = {A, C, E}, |N| = 3
  2. Is A core?3 ≥ 3 → yes, core
  3. Neighbourhood of CN(C) = {A, C, D, E}, |N| = 4 → core
  4. Neighbourhood of D — the interesting oned(D,C) = 1.5, exactly ≤ 1.5 ✓. Nothing else is within range. N(D) = {C, D}, |N| = 2 < 3 → not core
  5. So is D noise?No — D lies within ε of C, which is core → D is a border point
  6. Point HN(H) = {H} alone, |N| = 1, and no core point is within ε → noise
  7. Form the clusters from the core points{A, C, E} are mutually reachable → cluster 1, with D attached as border. {B, F, G} → cluster 2
Cluster 1 = {A, C, D, E}, cluster 2 = {B, F, G}, noise = {H}. k was never supplied. Note that D cannot pull anything further in, because border points do not expand a cluster.
Practice
  1. With ε = 2 and MinPts = 4, a point has 3 neighbours within ε (not counting itself). Is it core?
    Counting the point itself gives |N| = 4 ≥ 4 → yes, it is core. Forgetting to include the point itself is the commonest error in this topic.
  2. What happens to the output if you set ε far too large?
    Every point ends up in every other point's neighbourhood, so almost everything becomes core and they all connect → one single giant cluster and no noise. No error is raised. The opposite extreme, ε far too small, makes everything noise.
  3. Give one thing DBSCAN can do that k-Means cannot, and one thing k-Means does better.
    DBSCAN finds non-convex clusters (two interleaved crescents) and labels outliers as noise instead of forcing them into a cluster. k-Means is far faster on large data, handles high dimensions better, and gives you a centroid per cluster that you can interpret and reuse.

5Principal Component Analysis

Find the direction of greatest spread, make it the new first axis, and keep only the first few such directions.

Covariance and its eigenvectors
C = 1n−1 ∑ (xi−x̄)(xi−x̄)T,   Cu = λu
The eigenvalue is the variance along its eigenvector. For a 2×2 matrix, solve λ2 − (trace)λ + det = 0.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Standardise each feature unless they are already on comparable scales.
  2. Centre the data: subtract the mean of each column.
  3. Build the covariance matrix. For 2-D: var(x) and var(y) on the diagonal, cov(x, y) off it. Divide by n − 1.
  4. Solve det(C − λI) = 0 for the eigenvalues, largest first.
  5. For each λ, solve (C − λI)u = 0 and normalise u to unit length.
  6. Explained variance of component k = λk /  ∑ λj.
  7. Project: the new coordinate of a centred point is its dot product with u.
Worked example — PCA on five points, start to finish
Points (1,1) (2,3) (3,2) (4,5) (5,4). Both features are on the same scale already.
  1. Centre on the meanx̄ = (3, 3) → (−2,−2) (−1, 0) (0, −1) (1, 2) (2, 1)
  2. Variances (divide by n−1 = 4)var(x) = 4+1+0+1+44 = 2.5;  var(y) = 4+0+1+4+14 = 2.5
  3. Covariancecov = (−2)(−2) + 0 + 0 + (1)(2) + (2)(1)4 = 84 = 2.0
  4. Characteristic equation (trace 5, det 2.5² − 2² = 2.25)λ2 − 5λ + 2.25 = 0
  5. Solveλ = 5 ± √25 − 92 = 5 ± 42 → 4.5 and 0.5
  6. First eigenvector(2.5 − 4.5)u1 + 2u2 = 0 → u1 = u2 → (0.707, 0.707), the 45° line
  7. Explained variance4.54.5 + 0.5 = 90%
  8. Project (−2, −2) onto PC1(−2)(0.707) + (−2)(0.707) = −2.828
λ1 = 4.5 (90%), λ2 = 0.5 (10%). One dimension keeps nine-tenths of the information. Reconstructing from PC1 alone incurs a total squared error of exactly 4λ2 = 2.0 — the discarded eigenvalue is the error, known in advance.
Practice
  1. A covariance matrix has trace 10 and determinant 16. What are its eigenvalues?
    λ2 − 10λ + 16 = 0 → λ = (10 ± √100−64)/2 = (10 ± 6)/2 → 8 and 2. PC1 explains 8/10 = 80%.
  2. Eigenvalues are 6.0, 2.5, 1.0, 0.4, 0.1. How many components for 90% of the variance?
    Total = 10.0. Cumulative: 6.0 (60%), 8.5 (85%), 9.5 (95%). Two components give only 85%, so you need 3 components to clear 90%.
  3. Why must you standardise before PCA when features have different units?
    PCA maximises variance, and variance carries units. A feature measured in grams has 1000² times the variance of the same feature in kilograms, so it will dominate PC1 purely because of the unit choice. The result would be arithmetically correct and scientifically meaningless.

6Association rules: support, confidence, lift

From baskets, find the co-occurrences worth acting on — and discard the ones that are merely frequent.

The three measures
supp(A) = count(A)N,  conf = supp(A∪B)supp(A),  lift = confsupp(B)
Lift is the one that decides. Confidence alone is inflated by any consequent that is common anyway.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Count how many baskets contain the antecedent A.
  2. Count how many contain both A and B.
  3. Support = (both) / (total baskets).
  4. Confidence = (both) / (A alone) — note this divides by a count, not by the total.
  5. Compute supp(B) on its own.
  6. Lift = confidence / supp(B).
  7. Judge: lift > 1 real, = 1 independent, < 1 negatively associated. Report lift alongside confidence, never confidence alone.
Worked example — two rules with identical confidence and opposite meanings
Seven baskets: {E,J} {B,J,M,U} {E,U} {E,M,U} {B,J,M,U} {B,J,U} {J,M}. B = Bread, J = Jam, U = Butter, M = Milk, E = Eggs.
  1. Counts firstB in 3 baskets, J in 5, U in 5, M in 4, E in 3. {J,U} together in 3; {B,J,U} together in 3
  2. Rule 1: {Bread} → {Jam, Butter} — support37 = 0.429
  3. Its confidence33 = 1.000 — every Bread basket also had Jam and Butter
  4. Its liftsupp({J,U}) = 3/7 = 0.429, so lift = 1.0000.429 = 2.33
  5. Rule 2: {Jam} → {Butter} — confidence35 = 0.600
  6. Its liftsupp(U) = 5/7 = 0.714, so lift = 0.6000.714 = 0.84
  7. Rule 3: {Jam} → {Bread} — same confidence, different liftconf = 3/5 = 0.600; supp(B) = 3/7 = 0.429 → lift = 0.6000.429 = 1.40
Rules 2 and 3 have identical confidence (0.600) and opposite verdicts. Butter is in 5 of 7 baskets anyway, so Jam makes it less likely (lift 0.84); Bread is in only 3, so Jam genuinely predicts it (lift 1.40). Sort by confidence and you act on the wrong one.
Practice
  1. In 1000 transactions, Milk appears in 600, Bread in 500, and both in 400. Compute support, confidence and lift for {Milk} → {Bread}.
    supp = 400/1000 = 0.40. conf = 400/600 = 0.667. supp(Bread) = 500/1000 = 0.5, so lift = 0.667/0.5 = 1.33 — a genuine positive association, 33% above chance.
  2. A rule has confidence 0.95 and lift 0.99. Should you act on it?
    No. Lift ≈ 1 means the two items are essentially independent — the 0.95 confidence is simply because the consequent appears in about 95% of all baskets anyway. Knowing the antecedent tells you nothing you did not already know.
  3. Why is a rule with support 0.001 usually ignored even if its lift is 12?
    Because it describes about 1 basket in 1000. The lift may be real, but the rule is too rare to act on commercially, and with so few supporting baskets the estimate is statistically fragile — a handful of coincidences can produce it.
