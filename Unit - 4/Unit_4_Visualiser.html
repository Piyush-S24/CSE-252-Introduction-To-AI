1What unsupervised learning is

In supervised learning somebody hands you the answers and you learn the mapping. In unsupervised learning nobody does — you are given the inputs alone and asked what structure is in them.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Labels are expensive. Someone has to sit down and mark ten thousand emails as spam or not. Unlabelled data is free and already sitting in your database — so methods that need no labels are the only ones that can be applied at the scale most data actually exists at.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just label some of it and use supervised learning?” Often you should. But for some questions there is nothing to label: nobody can tell you the “correct” customer segment, because the segments are what you are trying to discover. Asking a human to label them first would mean already knowing the answer.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You treat the algorithm's output as a discovery rather than a proposal. k-Means with k = 3 returns three clusters on any data, including pure noise, and it reports them with exactly the same confidence either way.

Supervised

Data is (xi, yi) pairs. There is a right answer, so there is an error to minimise and a test score to report.

Unsupervised

Data is xi only. There is no right answer, so success is judged by whether the structure found is useful — which is why evaluation is genuinely harder.

TaskWhat it findsExample on the syllabus

The honest caveat
Run k-Means with k = 3 on data with no real groups and it will still return three clusters, perfectly confidently. An unsupervised algorithm always produces output; whether that output means anything is a separate question, and Unit V is where you learn to ask it.
Worked example — telling the three tasks apart from the data alone
Four datasets arrive. Decide what kind of learning each one supports — which is determined entirely by what feedback is available, not by what you would like to do.
  1. 10 000 emails, each already marked spam / not spamevery row carries its answer → supervised (classification)
  2. 2 million customer records, no annotations of any kindno answers exist → unsupervised (clustering)
  3. A robot arm, plus a score after each attempted graspno correct action is known, only a reward afterwards → reinforcement
  4. 500 labelled X-rays and 50 000 unlabelled onesa little supervision plus a lot of structure → semi-supervised
  5. The test that decides it in every caseask: for a single training example, do I have the answer? If yes, supervised. If no but I can score an action, reinforcement. If no and I cannot, unsupervised.
Nothing here depended on the algorithm. The feedback available decides the task, and the task then decides which algorithms are even candidates.

2k-Means clustering

Pick k centres, assign every point to its nearest centre, move each centre to the mean of what it got, and repeat until nothing moves.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

k-Means is the first clustering method to try because it is fast, simple and scales to millions of points. Each iteration is two easy steps, and the objective it minimises — total squared distance to the cluster centre — can only go down, so it always terminates.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just look at a scatter plot?” That works in two dimensions and stops working in ten, which is where most real data lives. “Why not try every possible grouping?” The number of ways to split n points into k groups is astronomically large — k-Means finds a good local answer in a handful of passes instead.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You take the first run as the answer. k-Means converges to a local minimum that depends on where the centres started, so two runs on identical data can give different clusters. Running it once and reporting the result is the most common way to get a worse answer than the data supports — which is why k-means++ and multiple restarts exist.

Step 1: assignment
assign:  Ck = [xi :  ‖xi − μk‖ ≤ ‖xi − μj‖  for all j]
Each point goes to whichever centre is closest — ties broken arbitrarily.
Step 2: update
update:  μk = 1|Ck| ∑xi ∈ Ck xi
Each centre moves to the average of the points that chose it. These two steps alternate, and each one can only lower the objective below.
The objective
SSE = K∑k=1  ∑xi ∈ Ck ‖xi − μk‖2
What k-Means actually minimises — the within-cluster sum of squared distances. It falls at every step, which is why the algorithm always terminates.

Lloyd's algorithm on 7 points, k = 2

The two centres start sitting on points A and D — a deliberately lopsided initialisation, so you can watch them pull apart and see that the first assignment is not the final one.

Three things that bite
k must be chosen in advance and the algorithm cannot tell you it was wrong. The result depends on the initialisation — a bad start converges to a worse SSE, which is why k-means++ and multiple restarts exist. It assumes round, equally-sized clusters, because the only thing it ever computes is distance to a centre.
Worked example — one full iteration of Lloyd's algorithm
Seven points A(1,1) B(1.5,2) C(3,4) D(5,7) E(3.5,5) F(4.5,5) G(3.5,4.5), with k = 2 and the centres deliberately started on A and D.
  1. Step 1 — measure every point to both centrese.g. for C(3,4): to μ1(1,1) is √(3−1)2 + (4−1)2 = √13 = 3.606; to μ2(5,7) is √4 + 9 = 3.606 as well
  2. Break the tie and assign everythingC is exactly equidistant, so the tie-break sends it to cluster 1. Result: cluster 1 = {A, B, C}, cluster 2 = {D, E, F, G}
  3. Step 2 — move each centre to the mean of its membersμ1 = (1 + 1.5 + 33, 1 + 2 + 43) = (1.833, 2.333)
  4. And the second centreμ2 = (5 + 3.5 + 4.5 + 3.54, 7 + 5 + 5 + 4.54) = (4.125, 5.375)
  5. Repeat until nothing movesthe next round reassigns C to cluster 2, and after that the centres settle at (1.25, 1.5) and (3.9, 5.1)
  6. Final within-cluster sum of squaresSSE = 8.525
Note step 2: the centre that started on A travelled all the way to (1.25, 1.5). The first assignment is never the final one — and point C, sitting on the boundary, changed cluster between iterations.

3Hierarchical clustering

Instead of committing to k up front, build the whole nesting: start with every point its own cluster and repeatedly merge the two closest.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Hierarchical clustering refuses to commit to k. It builds the entire nesting of groupings — from every point alone up to one big cluster — and lets you choose afterwards by cutting the tree wherever the structure looks real.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just use k-Means?” Because k-Means demands k before it will tell you anything, and often k is exactly what you do not know. The dendrogram also shows you how much structure there is at each level, which a flat clustering cannot. The cost is O(n2) memory, so it does not scale to millions of points the way k-Means does.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You pick a linkage without thinking and get a different answer. Single linkage chains long thin groups together; complete linkage forces compact balls. On the same five points these produce different trees — so quoting “the hierarchical clustering” without naming the linkage is meaningless.

LinkageDistance between clustersTends to produce
Singlenearest pair of memberslong chains; follows thin structures
Completefurthest pair of memberscompact, roughly equal clusters
Averagemean over all member pairsa compromise between the two

Linkage:
Reading a dendrogram
The height at which two clusters join is the distance at which they merged. To get a flat clustering, cut the tree horizontally — the number of vertical lines your cut crosses is the number of clusters. A cut through a tall gap is a more defensible clustering than one through a crowded region.
Worked example — two linkages on the same five points, step by step
P(7, 3.5) Q(9.5, 5) R(5, 9.5) S(5, 1.5) T(2, 4.5). Key distances: d(P,S) = 2.828, d(P,Q) = 2.915, d(P,T) = 5.099, d(Q,R) = 6.364, d(R,S) = 8.000.
  1. Both linkages start identically — merge the closest pairthe smallest distance anywhere is d(P, S) = 2.828 → merge to (PS)
  2. Single linkage, step 2: distance to a cluster is its nearest memberd(Q, (PS)) = min(2.915, 5.701) = 2.915 — the smallest remaining, so Q joins → (Q(PS))
  3. Complete linkage, step 2: distance is the furthest memberd(Q, (PS)) = max(2.915, 5.701) = 5.701, but d(T, (PS)) = max(5.099, 4.243) = 5.099 is smaller → T joins instead, giving (T(PS))
  4. Follow each to the endsingle: P+S, then Q, then T, then R — one point at a time, a chain
    complete: P+S, then T, then Q+R forms separately, then the two groups merge
  5. Read off a 2-cluster solution by cutting each treesingle gives {P,S,Q,T} and {R}; complete gives {T,P,S} and {Q,R}
Same points, same distances, two different answers — and complete linkage's is the more balanced one. That difference is entirely the linkage rule, which is why it has to be stated.

4DBSCAN — density-based clustering

Clusters are dense regions separated by sparse ones. Nothing is assumed about shape, k is never specified, and points in no dense region are simply labelled noise.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

DBSCAN defines a cluster as a dense region rather than as a ball around a centre. That one change buys three things k-Means cannot offer: clusters of any shape, no need to choose k, and an explicit label for points that belong nowhere.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not k-Means, it is faster?” k-Means assigns every point to a cluster, so a single far-off outlier drags a centre toward it and corrupts a real group. It also only ever produces round clusters, so two interleaved crescents come out cut in half. “Why not hierarchical?” It has no notion of noise either, and costs O(n2) memory.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Choose ε badly and you get one of two useless extremes: too small and every point is noise; too large and everything merges into one cluster. Neither failure raises an error — you simply get a labelling that means nothing.

The two definitions everything rests on
Nε(p) = [q ∈ D :  dist(p, q) ≤ ε],   p is core ⇔ |Nε(p)| ≥ minPts
The ε-neighbourhood includes the point itself, so a core point with minPts = 3 needs two other points within ε.

Core point

Has at least minPts points within ε (itself counted). These are the interior of a cluster.

Border point

Fewer than minPts neighbours, but lies within ε of some core point. It joins that core point's cluster but cannot grow it.

Noise point

Neither — not dense itself and not close to anything dense. DBSCAN is the only one of the three algorithms here that is allowed to say this point belongs nowhere.

Choosing ε and minPts
A common rule of thumb is minPts ≥ D + 1 where D is the number of dimensions. For ε, sort every point's distance to its minPts-th nearest neighbour, plot those sorted distances, and take the value at the sharp elbow. Too small and everything is noise; too large and everything merges into one cluster.
Worked example — classifying every point as core, border or noise
Eight points with ε = 1.5 and minPts = 3. A(8, 5.5) B(1.5, 8) C(8.5, 5) D(8.5, 3.5) E(7.5, 6) F(1.5, 9) G(2, 8.5) H(3.5, 2). The neighbourhood always includes the point itself.
  1. Point A — list everything within 1.5d(A,C) = √0.25 + 0.25 = 0.707, d(A,E) = 0.707, and A itself → N(A) = {A, C, E}, |N| = 3 ≥ 3 → core
  2. Point CN(C) = {A, C, D, E}, |N| = 4 ≥ 3 → core
  3. Point D — the interesting oned(D,C) = 1.5 ≤ 1.5 → C is in; nothing else is. N(D) = {C, D}, |N| = 2 < 3 → not core
  4. But D is not noise eitherD lies within ε of C, which is a core point → D is a border point and joins C's cluster
  5. Point HN(H) = {H} alone, |N| = 1, and no core point is within ε → noise
  6. Join the core points that are within ε of each other{A, C, E} form one cluster (D attaches as border); {B, F, G} form another
Two clusters, {A, C, D, E} and {B, F, G}, with H as noise. k was never supplied — the density decided how many there were. Note that a border point joins a cluster but cannot be used to extend it, which is why D does not pull anything else in.

5Principal Component Analysis

Find the direction along which the data varies most, and use it as the new first axis. Reduce dimensions by keeping only the first few such directions.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

PCA finds the directions along which your data actually varies, and lets you keep only those. It is fast, it is deterministic, and it tells you exactly what fraction of the information each component retains — so you can make the keep-or-drop decision on evidence rather than by feel.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just drop the columns that look unimportant?” Because information is usually spread across correlated columns rather than sitting in any one of them — two mediocre features can jointly carry what neither carries alone. PCA builds new axes from combinations instead of discarding originals.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Forget to standardise and PCA simply reports whichever feature has the biggest units. Salary in rupees will dominate age in years purely because the numbers are larger, and the first principal component will be “salary” — correct arithmetic, worthless result.

Covariance and its eigenvectors
C = 1n − 1n∑i=1 (xi − x̄)(xi − x̄)T,   Cu = λu
Centre the data, build the covariance matrix, then take its eigenvectors. The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is the first principal component, and its eigenvalue is the variance along that direction.
Explained variance
explained variance of PCk = λkd∑j=1 λj
How much of the total spread one component accounts for. Keep components until this cumulative fraction reaches whatever you are willing to settle for — 90% and 95% are the usual choices.

Rotate the axis and watch the variance

Five points, already centred. Drag the angle and watch the projected spread. The variance is maximised at exactly 45°, and that direction is the first principal component.

angle θ =20°
Why the eigenvector is the answer
The variance of the data projected onto a unit direction u is uTCu. Maximising that subject to ‖u‖ = 1 is exactly the eigenvalue problem Cu = λu, and the maximum value attained is the largest eigenvalue. So "the direction of greatest variance" and "the top eigenvector" are the same sentence written twice.
Scale first
PCA maximises variance, and variance has units. A feature measured in grams will dominate the same feature measured in kilograms. Standardise every feature to mean 0 and variance 1 before running PCA unless the features are already in comparable units.
Worked example — PCA on five points, computed by hand
Points (1,1) (2,3) (3,2) (4,5) (5,4). Both features are already on the same scale, so no standardisation is needed here.
  1. Centre the data on its mean̄x = (3, 3) → centred points (−2,−2) (−1,0) (0,−1) (1,2) (2,1)
  2. Build the 2×2 covariance matrix (divide by n − 1 = 4)var(x) = 4+1+0+1+44 = 2.5,  var(y) = 2.5,  cov = 4+0+0+2+24 = 2.0
  3. Write the characteristic equation det(C − λI) = 0(2.5 − λ)2 − 22 = 0  →  λ2 − 5λ + 2.25 = 0
  4. Solve for the eigenvaluesλ = 2.5 ± 2  →  λ1 = 4.5, λ2 = 0.5
  5. Find the first eigenvector(2.5 − 4.5)u1 + 2u2 = 0 → u1 = u2 → u = (0.7071, 0.7071), i.e. 45°
  6. Compute the explained variance4.54.5 + 0.5 = 90%
One dimension captures 90% of the spread. Drag the angle slider above to 45° and the measured variance reads 4.5 — exactly λ1, because the top eigenvalue is the variance along the top eigenvector.

6Association rule mining and Apriori

From a pile of shopping baskets, find the rules of the form people who buy these also buy that — and work out which of those rules are actually worth anything.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Transaction data is enormous, unlabelled and full of co-occurrences that are pure coincidence. Association rule mining is a disciplined way to separate the patterns worth acting on from the ones that merely happen often.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just count the most popular pairs?” Because the most frequent pairs are simply the most popular items, which you already knew. The value is in finding pairs that occur together more than their individual popularity explains — and that is what lift measures and raw counts cannot.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You rank rules by confidence and act on the top ones. A rule predicting something that happens in 5 of 7 baskets anyway will show high confidence for free, while being worse than useless — its lift is below 1, meaning the antecedent makes the consequent less likely.

Support and confidence
support(A) = baskets containing Aall baskets,    confidence(A → B) = support(A ∪ B)support(A)
Support is how often the combination happens at all. Confidence is how often B shows up given that A did.
Lift
lift(A → B) = confidence(A → B)support(B)
Lift is the one that matters. It compares the rule against pure chance. Lift > 1 means A makes B more likely; lift = 1 means they are independent and the rule is worthless however high its confidence; lift < 1 means A makes B less likely.

Apriori on seven baskets, minimum support 3

The Apriori principle: if an itemset is frequent, every subset of it is frequent too — so if any subset is infrequent, the itemset cannot possibly be frequent and never needs counting. That one observation is what makes the search tractable.

Why confidence alone will mislead you

Read the lift column first

Confidence alone cannot tell them apart, because a consequent that is common anyway will produce high confidence for free.
Worked example — computing support, confidence and lift for one rule
Seven baskets: {E,J} {B,J,M,U} {E,U} {E,M,U} {B,J,M,U} {B,J,U} {J,M}. B=Bread, J=Jam, U=Butter, M=Milk, E=Eggs. Test the rule {Bread} → {Jam, Butter}.
  1. Count how often Bread appearsbaskets 2, 5, 6 → supp(B) = 37 = 0.4286
  2. Count how often all three appear together{B, J, U} is in baskets 2, 5, 6 → supp(B ∪ J ∪ U) = 37 = 0.4286
  3. Confidence — of the Bread baskets, how many also had Jam and Butter?conf = supp(B, J, U)supp(B) = 3/73/7 = 1.000 — every single one
  4. Count the consequent on its own{J, U} together appear in baskets 2, 5, 6 → supp(J, U) = 37 = 0.4286
  5. Lift — compare the confidence against chancelift = confsupp(J, U) = 1.0000.4286 = 2.333
  6. Interpretlift > 1, so buying Bread makes Jam-and-Butter 2.3× more likely than the baseline rate — a real pattern, not a coincidence
Compare with {Jam} → {Butter} on the same data: confidence 0.600 but lift 0.84. Butter is in 5 of 7 baskets regardless, so knowing about Jam makes it less likely. Identical-looking confidence, opposite conclusions — which is the entire argument for reading lift.

7Unsupervised methods in robotics and sensor processing

Robots generate enormous quantities of unlabelled data and almost no labelled data, which is exactly the setting these methods were built for.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A robot generates enormous quantities of unlabelled sensor data every second and almost no labelled data at all. That asymmetry — not any property of the algorithms — is what makes unsupervised methods indispensable in robotics.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not label the sensor data?” A single LiDAR scan is tens of thousands of 3-D points, produced ten times a second. Labelling even one minute of operation by hand is infeasible, and the robot must work in environments nobody labelled in advance.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You use a method that cannot say “I do not know”. k-Means assigns every LiDAR return to some object, including returns from dust and rain — so the robot confidently perceives obstacles that are not there. DBSCAN's noise label is the whole reason it is preferred for this job.

Segmenting a LiDAR scan

A scan is tens of thousands of unlabelled 3-D points. DBSCAN groups them into objects without being told how many objects there are, and marks sparse returns from dust or rain as noise — both properties matter here, which is why it is preferred to k-Means for this job.

Compressing sensor streams

A robot with dozens of correlated sensors can have that stream reduced by PCA to a handful of components that keep most of the variance, cutting bandwidth and downstream computation.

Vector quantisation

k-Means over image patches or sensor readings builds a codebook, turning a continuous signal into a small set of discrete symbols that a planner can reason about.

Detecting a fault before it fails

Fit the density of normal operation; a reading that falls in no dense region is flagged. No examples of the failure are needed — which is the point, since the failures you most care about are the ones that have not happened yet.

The common thread
Every one of these works because labels are expensive and unlabelled data is free. That asymmetry, rather than any property of the algorithms, is what makes unsupervised learning indispensable in robotics.
Worked example — choosing the right method for three robotics tasks
Each task is unsupervised. The right choice follows from one property of the task, not from which algorithm is most sophisticated.
  1. Segment a LiDAR scan into objectsnumber of objects is unknown and varies every frame; dust gives sparse spurious returns → needs no-k and a noise label → DBSCAN
  2. Compress 40 correlated sensor channels before transmittingwant maximum information in fewest numbers, and it must be reversible → PCA, keeping enough components for ~95% variance
  3. Build a small vocabulary of recurring terrain patcheswant a fixed number of representative prototypes → k-Means, where the k centroids become the codebook
  4. Detect a failing motor before it failsno examples of the failure exist yet → fit the density of normal operation and flag whatever falls outside it
  5. The common threadin every case labels are unavailable or impossible — not merely inconvenient
Three tasks, three different algorithms, one reason: what the task needs decides the method. DBSCAN for shape and noise, PCA for reversible compression, k-Means for a fixed codebook.
